\begin{abstract}
Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across distributed clients without sharing raw data, but its reliance on client-submitted updates exposes it to Byzantine model-poisoning attacks. Existing defenses depend on statistical assumptions about the number of malicious clients, a clean server-side dataset, or single-statistic screens, and consequently fail under adaptive attacks or non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data. We propose USTAD-FL, an unsupervised spatio-temporal anomaly detection framework that removes malicious clients before aggregation without assuming the number of malicious clients, a clean reference dataset, or the attack type. Our key observation is that, however carefully malicious clients disguise individual updates, their sustained participation eventually degrades the global model, whereas benign clients---even under non-IID data---stabilize after a few rounds; this contrast appears as joint spatial and temporal irregularity in the update stream. USTAD-FL couples a Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory (ConvLSTM) forecaster with a low-cost statistical detector, and applies an Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) with selective re-evaluation to bound detection cost. On MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, USTAD-FL matches or exceeds three state-of-the-art defenses under a $40\%$ adversarial fraction and high heterogeneity; its detection rate is at least $0.999$ from the second round of the attack onward, and its selective re-evaluation policy removes approximately 34\% of all ConvLSTM forward passes at the recommended operating point. We further show that this saving is bought not with detection recall, which is invariant to the re-evaluation trigger, but with precision, and we identify the mechanism responsible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%=============================================================================


\IEEEPARstart{F}{ederated} learning (FL) has emerged as a cornerstone of privacy-preserving machine learning at the wireless edge and is widely regarded as a key enabler of distributed intelligence in sixth-generation (6G) networks~\cite{sandeepa2025federated}. By keeping raw data on client devices and exchanging only model updates with a central server, FL supports large-scale collaborative training across resource-constrained devices without centralizing sensitive data. However, the very properties that make FL attractive---decentralization and the server's lack of visibility into client data---also expose it to \emph{Byzantine} threats. Because the server observes only the submitted updates, a subset of compromised clients can upload arbitrarily crafted updates that degrade the accuracy of the global model or divert its convergence~\cite{baruch2019little, fang2020local, shejwalkar2021manipulating}. Such model-poisoning attacks are particularly damaging in FL: a small fraction of adversaries can collapse the global model, and an adversary can refine its strategy across rounds by exploiting the publicly broadcast global model.

Existing defenses against Byzantine clients fall into two broad families, both of which face fundamental limitations under realistic conditions. The first family comprises Byzantine-robust aggregators such as Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine}, the coordinate-wise median, and the trimmed mean~\cite{yin2018byzantine}, which discard or downweight statistical outliers. These rules rely on strong assumptions about the number of malicious clients, which is rarely known in practice, and they tend to misclassify benign-but-divergent updates under non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data. The second family assigns trust from richer signals: FLTrust~\cite{cao2020fltrust} requires a clean root dataset at the server to bootstrap a reference model, whereas FLDetector~\cite{zhang2022fldetector} tracks the consistency of update histories but incurs substantial computational cost and is unreliable when attacks begin in early rounds. Critically, both families remain vulnerable to \emph{adaptive}, aggregator-aware attacks such as the ``A Little Is Enough'' (LIE)~\cite{baruch2019little} and Min-Max~\cite{shejwalkar2021manipulating} strategies, which craft malicious updates that lie within the statistical support of benign ones and therefore evade any single-statistic screen.

These limitations point to a common gap: a Byzantine defense that operates \emph{without} prior knowledge of the adversary---neither the number of malicious clients, nor a clean server-side dataset, nor the attack type---while remaining robust under non-IID heterogeneity. Our approach is motivated by a key observation: regardless of how carefully an adversary disguises individual updates, sustained participation by malicious clients eventually degrades the global model, whereas honest clients---even under heterogeneous data---settle into stable behavior after a few communication rounds. This contrast is seldom reliable in any single update, but it manifests over time as a joint \emph{spatial} irregularity in the structure of an update and a \emph{temporal} inconsistency across rounds.

Building on this observation, we propose USTAD-FL, an unsupervised spatio-temporal anomaly-detection framework that filters malicious clients before aggregation. USTAD-FL projects each high-dimensional update into a compact latent space, encodes it as a multi-channel spatial feature frame, and scores every client with two complementary detectors: a low-cost per-round statistical detector that captures spatial irregularity from the first post-warm-up round, and a Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory (ConvLSTM)~\cite{shi2015convolutional} forecaster that captures temporal inconsistency once sufficient history has accumulated. The two scores are fused and compared against a dual adaptive-quantile/absolute threshold, after which only the trusted subset is aggregated. Because per-round ConvLSTM inference is expensive, we couple it with an Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA)~\cite{hunter1986exponentially} and a selective re-evaluation policy that concentrates computation on the most suspicious clients. We evaluate USTAD-FL against the Gaussian, LIE~\cite{baruch2019little}, and Min-Max~\cite{shejwalkar2021manipulating} attacks on two benchmarks and compare it with three state-of-the-art defenses spanning both families: Multi-Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine}, FLTrust~\cite{cao2020fltrust}, and FLDetector~\cite{zhang2022fldetector}.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose USTAD-FL, a server-side spatio-temporal anomaly-detection framework that excludes Byzantine clients before aggregation \emph{without} assuming the number of malicious clients, a clean root dataset, or the attack type, and that remains effective under non-IID heterogeneity.
    \item We design a dual-arm detector that fuses a five-signal per-round statistical detector with a ConvLSTM next-frame forecaster, so that the heterogeneous signatures of norm-inflating, sub-mean (LIE), and clone-type (Min-Max) attacks are jointly observable; a two-sided robust normalization further resolves the score-polarity inversion that LIE induces in the temporal detector.
    \item We introduce an EWMA-based adaptive scoring policy and a dual-threshold filter that expose the robustness--participation trade-off through a single strictness index and bound the detection cost, reducing ConvLSTM forward passes by approximately 34\%---and by up to 77\% at its most aggressive setting---with no measurable loss of accuracy and no loss of detection recall.
    \item We characterize what the compute saving actually costs. Sweeping the re-evaluation trigger over $66$ configurations, we find that recall is invariant to it (never below $0.983$), so throttling never lets attackers through; the price is instead paid in precision, through a rise in benign-client rejection. We trace this to a specific interaction between the decay of unscored clients' temporal scores and the low branch of the two-sided LIE detector, and state the correction it implies.
    \item We conduct an extensive evaluation on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST under three representative attacks, comprising a head-to-head comparison against three state-of-the-art defenses---showing that USTAD-FL matches or exceeds the strongest baseline, including Multi-Krum, which is granted privileged knowledge of the adversary count---together with a per-round detection and efficiency study, all under a 40\% adversarial fraction.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} reviews preliminaries and related work. Section~\ref{sec:formulation} formalizes the problem. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the proposed method. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Preliminaries and Related Work}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Federated Learning}

We consider a standard FL system with Federated Averaging (FedAvg)~\cite{mcmahan2017communication} comprising a central server~$S$ and a set of~$N$ clients. Each client~$k$ holds a private training dataset~$D_k$ with~$n_k$ samples, and $n_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} n_k$ denotes the total number of training samples across all clients. At communication round~$t$, the server broadcasts the current global parameter vector~$\mathbf{w}^t$ to the participating clients. Each client performs several steps of local training and returns its local update~$\mathbf{w}_{k,t+1}$ to the server, which then aggregates these updates by computing a weighted average of the client parameters according to their local dataset sizes. The FedAvg update rule is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}^{t+1} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{n_k}{n_{\text{total}}} \mathbf{w}_{k,t+1},
\label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}
where each client's contribution is weighted by its local dataset size. Recently, algorithms such as FedProx~\cite{li2020federated} and SCAFFOLD~\cite{karimireddy2021scaffold} have achieved better performance than FedAvg. However, they only modify the local update procedure at the client side while the server-side aggregator remains unchanged. Since our proposed method operates solely on the server side, it is compatible with any aggregation rule.

\subsection{Byzantine Attacks in Federated Learning}

While FL preserves the privacy of client data, it introduces a new security threat: compromised clients can submit arbitrary updates that degrade the global model's performance or divert its convergence in a harmful direction. Several studies~\cite{yin2018byzantine, shi2022challenges} have shown that manipulating client updates to craft malicious contributions can be highly effective at reducing global model performance. In the most extreme case, a single malicious client can significantly reduce the global model accuracy. Moreover, because FL training iterates over multiple rounds, malicious clients can gain additional information to craft malicious updates that closely resemble benign ones, thereby bypassing anomaly detection~\cite{baruch2019little}. In realistic deployments, non-IID data is a common challenge when coordinating multiple clients. Over multiple communication rounds, divergent client updates may confuse statistical robust aggregators~\cite{blanchard2017machine, yin2018byzantine}. To evaluate our solution, we define a Byzantine threat model along four dimensions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Byzantine goals:} We focus on \textit{convergence-prevention} (untargeted) attacks~\cite{baruch2019little, shejwalkar2021manipulating}, in which Byzantine clients seek to reduce the global model's accuracy across all inputs and thereby render it unusable. Targeted \textit{backdoor} attacks---which corrupt the model only on trigger-bearing inputs while preserving its overall accuracy---have a more localized footprint and are left as future work.

    \item \textbf{Byzantine knowledge:} The attacker may have knowledge about data, model updates (or gradients), and the aggregation rule. In~\cite{baruch2019little, fang2020local}, attackers possess full or partial knowledge of training data and model updates of all participating clients. We also consider the scenario where attackers possess certain benign data to optimize malicious update generation without detection~\cite{shejwalkar2021manipulating}. The attacker may know the aggregation rules and solve optimization problems to generate malicious updates that are harmful yet remain within acceptable limits.

    \item \textbf{Byzantine capability:} Attackers can manipulate client devices and interfere with the communication between clients and the server through Man-In-The-Middle (MITM) attacks. We assume that the attacker controls a subset of client devices, typically fewer than 50\% of the total clients.

    \item \textbf{Byzantine behavior:} In each communication round, malicious clients participate in the training process. To evaluate our solution under extreme conditions, we assume that in each communication round, all malicious clients submit their updates to maximize global model corruption.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Defender's perspective:} Since Byzantine attacks can be highly effective even with a small number of compromised clients, attackers can adaptively change their strategies due to the public nature of the global model. Our solution must achieve high detection accuracy while ensuring fast convergence without prior knowledge of the client dataset or the attacker's behavior.

\subsection{Related Work}

We group existing defenses against Byzantine clients into two families---\emph{robust aggregators} and \emph{trust/history-based detectors}---and summarize their assumptions and capabilities in Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison}. Robust Aggregator (RA) methods replace the FedAvg mean with a statistically robust rule, building on the premise that honest gradients cluster together whereas malicious gradients deviate significantly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Krum}~\cite{blanchard2017machine}: Instead of averaging all client gradients as in FedAvg, Krum selects the single most reliable gradient based on the Euclidean distance between gradients. Krum is resilient to Byzantine behavior and robust to outliers.

    \item \textbf{Multi-Krum}~\cite{blanchard2017machine}: An extension of Krum that selects multiple reliable gradients and averages them. It improves convergence speed while retaining Byzantine resistance.

    \item \textbf{Median}~\cite{yin2018byzantine}: A robust statistical method that uses the coordinate-wise median instead of the mean. Since the median is not affected by outliers, malicious gradients with extremely large or small values do not significantly impact the aggregation result.

    \item \textbf{Trimmed Mean}~\cite{yin2018byzantine}: Another robust statistical method removes gradients with extremely large or small values, then averages the remaining values. This process eliminates anomalous gradients submitted by Byzantine clients.
\end{itemize}

Although these methods are effective against certain Byzantine attacks, they require strong assumptions about the number of malicious clients. These assumptions are usually impractical in the real world and may lead to information loss if they are not accurate. Moreover, Krum and Multi-Krum are robust but suffer from slow convergence, whereas Median and Trimmed Mean remain susceptible to stealthy attacks that stay within their filtering bounds.

Another approach is to limit or eliminate the impact of Byzantine clients by assigning weights to updates. The server evaluates client model updates to determine their impact on the global model. Suspicious clients with negative contributions are removed from aggregation or assigned low weights.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FLTrust}~\cite{cao2020fltrust}: A robust aggregation rule that assigns a trust score to each model update to minimize the impact of malicious updates. FLTrust requires a clean root dataset at the server side to train a reference model. In each communication round, the server aligns each client's update with the reference model using cosine similarity. The server also normalizes client updates to match the reference model magnitude, preventing malicious clients from influencing aggregation through large updates. The final global update is a weighted average of the normalized client updates with weights based on client trust scores.

    \item \textbf{FLDetector}~\cite{zhang2022fldetector}: A Byzantine attack detection method based on anomalies in client update history. The server uses the Cauchy mean-value theorem and the L-BFGS algorithm~\cite{liu1989limited} to estimate the Hessian matrix of each client update, thereby predicting the update each client should send in the next communication. It then compares client update magnitudes to match the server's received update and the predicted update, and assigns a suspicion score to identify malicious clients. FLDetector demonstrates good performance, but it is less reliable when attacks begin in early training rounds.
\end{itemize}

These trust- and history-based detectors exploit deeper structure in client updates and are therefore better at identifying Byzantine patterns under non-IID data than purely geometric rules. However, they still struggle against large malicious populations and adaptive attacks, and FLDetector in particular requires a long benign update history and therefore reacts slowly to poisoning that begins before that history is established.

Finally, we formulate the Byzantine-robust learning task as a constrained optimization problem, which is motivated by the spatio-temporal analysis developed in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{System and Communication Model}

We consider a group of $N$ clients indexed by $\mathcal{K} = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$, collaborating with a global server. Local data are distributed across clients in a heterogeneous, non-IID distribution; each client $k \in \mathcal{K}$ holds a private dataset $D_k$ whose label distribution follows a Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameter $\alpha$, a standard proxy for real-world data heterogeneity in federated settings~\cite{hsieh2020non}. To accommodate the inherent constraints, only a fraction $\kappa \in (0, 1]$ of the clients participate in each communication round.

Let $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the $d$-dimensional parameter vector of the shared global model $f_{\mathbf{w}}(\cdot)$. At the beginning of round~$t$, the BS broadcasts $\mathbf{w}^t$ to the participating set $S_t \subseteq \mathcal{K}$. Each client $k \in S_t$ then performs $E$ epochs of mini-batch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with learning rate~$\eta$ on its local dataset $D_k$, producing the updated parameter vector $\mathbf{w}_{k,t+1}$, which is subsequently uploaded to the BS.

\subsection{Federated Learning Objective}

Let $n_k = |D_k|$, $n_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} n_k$, and $n_t = \sum_{k \in S_t} n_k$ denote, respectively, the local sample size of client~$k$, the global sample size, and the round-$t$ participating sample size. The global FL objective is to minimize the data-weighted aggregate of local empirical risks, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d} F(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{n_k}{n_{\text{total}}} F_k(\mathbf{w}),
\label{eq:global_obj}
\end{equation}
where the local empirical risk of client~$k$ is given by
\begin{equation}
F_k(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n_k} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_k} \ell(f_{\mathbf{w}}(x), y),
\label{eq:local_risk}
\end{equation}
with $\ell(\cdot, \cdot)$ being a task-specific loss function (e.g., cross-entropy in the case of classification). At each communication round~$t$, the BS advances toward~\eqref{eq:global_obj} by collecting the locally updated parameter vectors $\{\mathbf{w}_{k,t+1}\}_{k \in S_t}$ and applying a server-side aggregation rule $\text{AGG}(\cdot)$, namely,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}^{t+1} = \text{AGG}\left(\{(\mathbf{w}_{k,t+1}, n_k)\}_{k \in S_t}\right).
\label{eq:aggregation}
\end{equation}
It is worth noting that the choice of $\text{AGG}(\cdot)$ is intentionally left abstract at this stage so as to accommodate the broad family of aggregators studied in the literature, including the data-weighted mean of FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}, the distance-based selection of Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine}, the coordinate-wise trimmed mean and median rules~\cite{yin2018byzantine}, and trust-weighted aggregators such as FLTrust~\cite{cao2020fltrust}. Regardless of the specific rule adopted, \eqref{eq:aggregation} is iterated across $T_{\text{total}}$ rounds with the expectation that $\mathbf{w}^{T_{\text{total}}}$ converges to a neighborhood of the minimizer of~\eqref{eq:global_obj}.

\subsection{Byzantine Threat Model}

We assume that an adversary can compromise up to $M < N/2$ of the clients in $\mathcal{K}$. The compromised clients comply with the FL protocol at the message-exchange level but are free to upload arbitrarily crafted parameter vectors in place of those produced by honest local training, while the remaining $N - M$ clients behave honestly. Let $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{K}$ denote the malicious set, with $|\mathcal{M}| = M$. In this work, we further assume that $\mathcal{M}$ is fixed at the onset of the attack phase, following an initial warm-up of $T_w$ rounds during which all clients are honest. Such an assumption is consistent with the threat models adopted in~\cite{baruch2019little, shejwalkar2021manipulating}.

Following~\cite{shejwalkar2021manipulating, baruch2019little}, the adversary is treated as omniscient: at each round~$t$, it has full knowledge of the current global model $\mathbf{w}^t$ and of the local updates submitted by all participants in $S_t$. Let $\mathbf{g}_{k,t} = \mathbf{w}_{k,t+1}$ denote the update transmitted by client~$k$ in round~$t$. The vectors received by the BS under the Byzantine attack can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t} =
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{g}_{k,t} & \text{if } k \notin \mathcal{M}, \\
\mathcal{A}(G_t, \mathbf{w}^t, k) & \text{if } k \in \mathcal{M},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:byzantine_update}
\end{equation}
where $G_t = \{\mathbf{g}_{j,t} : j \in S_t\}$ collects all local updates and $\mathcal{A}(\cdot)$ denotes the attack function. Substituting~\eqref{eq:byzantine_update} into a data-weighted mean aggregator, the BS produces the poisoned global model
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{t+1} = \sum_{k \in S_t} \frac{n_k}{n_t} \hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t}.
\label{eq:poisoned_agg}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Byzantine Attack Strategies}

We consider three representative instantiations of $\mathcal{A}(\cdot)$, ranging from a simple stochastic perturbation to highly engineered model-poisoning strategies. To simplify the exposition, let $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t = \frac{1}{|B_t|} \sum_{j \in B_t} \mathbf{g}_{j,t}$ denote the coordinate-wise mean of the benign updates and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t$ their coordinate-wise standard deviation, where $B_t = S_t \setminus \mathcal{M}$ is the honest participant set in round~$t$.

\subsubsection{Gaussian Noise Attack}
Under this attack, each Byzantine client independently perturbs its local update by adding zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma_g > 0$, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t} = \mathbf{g}_{k,t} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_g^2 \mathbf{I}_d).
\label{eq:gaussian_attack}
\end{equation}
Although straightforward to launch, the effectiveness of this attack diminishes rapidly as $\sigma_g$ grows beyond the typical norm range of benign updates: such updates become statistically distinguishable from honest contributions and can be flagged by even simple norm-based screens.

\subsubsection{Little-Is-Enough (LIE) Attack~\cite{baruch2019little}}
The LIE attack is a far more sophisticated strategy that remains statistically concealed within the natural variation of honest updates while still steering the aggregated model away from convergence. Rather than injecting conspicuously large perturbations, every client in $\mathcal{M}$ transmits the same engineered vector, which is given by
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - z_{\max} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t, \quad \forall\, k \in \mathcal{M},
\label{eq:lie_attack}
\end{equation}
where $z_{\max}$ denotes the largest perturbation scale that preserves the statistical plausibility of the malicious updates. Letting $s = \lfloor N/2 \rfloor + 1 - M$ denote the minimum number of supporters required to pass majority-based screening, $z_{\max}$ can be calculated as
\begin{equation}
z_{\max} = \Phi\left(\frac{N - M - s}{N - M}\right),
\label{eq:zmax}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. It is worth noting that LIE is particularly damaging against aggregators that rely on coordinate-wise statistical filtering, since the malicious updates are deliberately positioned within the retained support of the benign distribution.

\subsubsection{Min-Max Agnostic Attack~\cite{shejwalkar2021manipulating}}
The Min-Max attack is an aggregator-agnostic poisoning strategy that maximizes the magnitude of the adversarial perturbation subject to a detectability constraint. The attack chooses a perturbation direction $\mathbf{p} = -\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t$ and seeks the largest scalar $\gamma > 0$ such that the corrupted updates remain geometrically indistinguishable from the benign ones, which can be formulated as
\begin{equation}
\max_{\gamma} \;\; \gamma \quad \text{s.t.} \;\; \max_{j \in B_t} \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_t + \gamma \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{g}_{j,t}\| \leq \max_{i,j \in B_t} \|\mathbf{g}_{i,t} - \mathbf{g}_{j,t}\|.
\label{eq:minmax_attack}
\end{equation}
Each Byzantine client then submits $\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t + \gamma^* \mathbf{p}$, where $\gamma^*$ is the optimal solution to~\eqref{eq:minmax_attack}. Since the constraint forces the malicious updates to lie within the convex hull of the benign cluster, the Min-Max attack is particularly difficult to detect using geometric or distance-based defenses. Notably, an additional structural side-effect of~\eqref{eq:minmax_attack} is that all Byzantine clients converge to the same engineered vector, so they appear as near-identical clones at the BS.



\subsection{Byzantine-Robust FL Problem}
\label{sec:p1}

Motivated by the above analysis, we cast the Byzantine-robust learning task as the joint design of (i) a per-round decision rule that identifies a trusted participant subset $\widetilde{S}_t \subseteq S_t$, and (ii) a server-side aggregator $\text{AGG}(\cdot)$ that operates on $\widetilde{S}_t$ rather than on the full participant set $S_t$. Letting $\mathbf{w}^{T_{\text{total}}}(\widetilde{S}_{1:T_{\text{total}}})$ denote the global model produced after $T_{\text{total}}$ rounds under a sequence of trusted subsets $\widetilde{S}_{1:T_{\text{total}}}=\{\widetilde{S}_t\}_{t=1}^{T_{\text{total}}}$, the optimization problem can be expressed as
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:p1}
\begin{align}
(\mathrm{P1}):\quad\min_{\widetilde{S}_{1:T_{\text{total}}}}\;& F\!\left(\mathbf{w}^{T_{\text{total}}}(\widetilde{S}_{1:T_{\text{total}}})\right) \label{eq:p1_obj}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad &\widetilde{S}_t \subseteq S_t,\quad \forall\,t\in\{1,\dots,T_{\text{total}}\}, \tag{P1a}\\
&|\widetilde{S}_t| \ge S_{\min},\quad \forall\,t, \tag{P1b}\\
&\widetilde{S}_t \cap \mathcal{M} = \emptyset \quad \text{(ideal Byzantine-free)}, \tag{P1c}\\
&\mathbf{w}^{t+1}=\text{AGG}\!\left(\{(\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t},n_k)\}_{k\in\widetilde{S}_t}\right),\tag{P1d}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where (P1a) restricts the trusted subset to clients that actually participate in round~$t$, (P1b) enforces a minimum participation level $S_{\min}$ to keep the aggregator statistically meaningful, (P1c) is the idealized Byzantine-free condition, and (P1d) couples consecutive rounds through the aggregation rule.

Solving Problem (P1) is challenging for three reasons. First, the malicious set $\mathcal{M}$ is unknown to the BS; consequently, (P1c) is generally not directly verifiable and must be replaced by a data-driven proxy. Second, the attack strategies introduced in~\eqref{eq:gaussian_attack}--\eqref{eq:minmax_attack} exhibit fundamentally different statistical signatures: Gaussian noise inflates the update norm, Min-Max forces the malicious clients to behave as clones near the benign centroid, and LIE produces a fixed sub-mean vector that is, by design, locally indistinguishable from the benign distribution. Hence, no single descriptor—be it norm, distance, or directional similarity—is sufficient to flag all three families simultaneously. Third, the operational constraints of low-resource networks preclude both per-round brute-force scoring of every client and the use of computationally heavy verification primitives; accordingly, the BS must allocate its detection budget adaptively over rounds and clients.

In particular, conventional deterministic optimization methods are difficult to apply to Problem (P1) because the trusted-subset variables $\widetilde{S}_{1:T_{\text{total}}}$ are combinatorial, the malicious-set indicator is hidden, and the per-round screening must be carried out under tight latency constraints at the BS. Fortunately, recent advances in convolutional recurrent learning have shown that ConvLSTM~\cite{shi2015convolutional} architectures are an effective tool for capturing joint spatial and temporal regularities in high-dimensional sequential signals. Hence, in this work, we propose a spatio-temporal anomaly-detection framework, presented in Section~\ref{sec:method}, that (i) jointly models the spatial structure and temporal evolution of the client updates through a ConvLSTM forecaster, (ii) complements it with a low-cost five-signal statistical detector that tolerates the LIE polarity inversion through a two-sided MAD-based normalization, and (iii) embeds both detectors into a dual-threshold adaptive filter that exposes the trade-off between robustness and participation as a single tunable strictness index.

%=============================================================================
\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}
%=============================================================================

To address Problem (P1), we develop an \emph{Unsupervised Spatio-Temporal Anomaly Detection} framework for federated learning (USTAD-FL). The proposed framework is motivated by two observations drawn from the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}. First, Byzantine behaviors cannot, in general, be reliably identified from a single update because of client heterogeneity and the non-IID nature of the local datasets. Second, malicious updates exhibit two complementary kinds of irregularity: a \emph{spatial} abnormality in their parameter pattern and a \emph{temporal} inconsistency across communication rounds. Accordingly, the proposed framework jointly models the structural properties of client updates and their temporal evolution, so that the trusted subset $\widetilde{S}_t$ in (P1) is identified from a fused, two-arm anomaly score whose strictness adapts to the empirical threat level of each round.

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the end-to-end workflow of the proposed framework.

\subsection{Framework Overview}

At each federated round~$t$, the BS broadcasts the current global model $\mathbf{w}^t$ to the participating clients. Each client performs local training and returns its update $\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t}$ to the BS. Instead of directly aggregating the received updates, the BS first transforms each update into a compact spatio-temporal representation, computes an anomaly score for each client through two complementary detectors, fuses the two scores, and finally aggregates only the trusted subset $\widetilde{S}_t$. Accordingly, the proposed framework consists of five stages, summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{SVD latent projection:} Extract the layer-filtered update of each client and project it into a fixed low-dimensional latent space using a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) basis fitted from clean warm-up rounds.
    \item \textbf{Spatio-temporal feature construction:} Encode each client's latent update into a multi-channel spatial feature frame and maintain a per-client sliding temporal window.
    \item \textbf{Per-round statistical anomaly detection:} Score each round on five complementary signals, namely norm inflation, cosine deviation, MAD-normalized distance from the coordinate-wise median, signed disagreement, and pairwise clone similarity, and combine them into a single statistical anomaly score.
    \item \textbf{ConvLSTM-EWMA temporal scoring:} A lightweight ConvLSTM forecaster predicts the next feature frame of each client; the squared prediction error is EWMA-smoothed and subsequently normalized against the per-round population distribution to yield a two-sided relative score that is robust to the polarity inversion induced by the LIE attack.
    \item \textbf{Score fusion and dual-threshold aggregation:} The two detector arms are fused by max-pooling, and the resulting fused score is compared against the smaller of an adaptive quantile threshold and a fixed absolute threshold, after which the trusted subset $\widetilde{S}_t$ is aggregated.
\end{enumerate}

In what follows, we elaborate on each stage and summarize the overall procedure in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ustad}.

\subsection{SVD Latent Projection}

High-dimensional model updates $\mathbf{g}_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are prohibitively expensive to process directly with spatial neural networks. To reduce dimensionality while retaining the directions of maximum variation among client updates, we apply a fixed data-driven projection based on the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)~\cite{zhang2015singular}, with the basis learned exclusively from benign updates collected during the $T_w$-round warm-up phase.

\subsubsection{Basis Fitting}
Let $\mathcal{L} \subset \{1, \ldots, d\}$ denote a selected subset of model layers. In our implementation $\mathcal{L}$ comprises the fully-connected head of the client model, which carries the most task-relevant information while keeping $D$ two orders of magnitude below the full parameter count ($D = 66{,}954$ for the CNN used here). For each participating client $k$ and the current global model $\mathbf{w}^t$, the layer-filtered update vector can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,t}^{(\mathcal{L})} = \bigl(\mathbf{w}_{k,t+1}^{(\ell)} - \mathbf{w}_t^{(\ell)}\bigr)_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}} \in \mathbb{R}^D,
\label{eq:layer_update}
\end{equation}
where $D = \sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}} |\mathbf{w}^{(\ell)}|$ is the total number of parameters in the selected layers. During warm-up, all incoming $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,t}^{(\mathcal{L})}$ are treated as benign and collected into a bootstrap matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_b \times D}$, where $n_b$ denotes the number of accumulated samples. The basis is fitted once, as soon as $n_b \geq n_{\min}$; since all $N$ clients report in the first round and $N > n_{\min}$, this occurs at $t = 1$, well inside the clean warm-up window and hence on uncorrupted updates only. The matrix is then centered as
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^\top, \quad \boldsymbol{\mu} = \frac{1}{n_b} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{1},
\label{eq:centering}
\end{equation}
and compute its SVD:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top, \quad \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times n_b}.
\label{eq:svd}
\end{equation}
We retain the top-$K$ right singular vectors to form the projection basis $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{V}_{:K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times D}$. The target latent dimension is the grid size $HW$, so that the projected coordinates tile a spatial map of height~$H$ and width~$W$; the attainable dimension is, however, capped by the rank of the bootstrap matrix, giving
\begin{equation}
K = \min\bigl(HW,\; n_b\bigr).
\label{eq:latent_dim}
\end{equation}
In our setting $n_b = N = 100$ and $HW = 256$, hence $K = 100$: the basis is rank-limited by the number of warm-up samples rather than by the grid. The latent code is zero-padded from $K$ to $HW$ before reshaping, so that $HW - K$ cells of the spatial map are structurally inactive. Both $\mathbf{B}$ and the sample mean~$\boldsymbol{\mu}$ are fixed after fitting and reused unchanged for all subsequent rounds.

\subsubsection{Runtime Projection}
At each round $t > T_w$, the BS projects every incoming update into the fixed latent space, namely,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{k,t} = \mathbf{B}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,t}^{(\mathcal{L})} - \boldsymbol{\mu}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^K.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
The projection yields a compact latent code of dimension $K \ll D$ that captures the dominant directions of update variation learned from clean warm-up data. Notably, Byzantine updates that deviate from the benign manifold project far from the origin in this latent space, which renders downstream anomaly detection significantly more reliable.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Feature Construction}

To enable a convolutional architecture to capture the structured co-variation of the latent updates, the latent code $\mathbf{z}_{k,t}$ is reshaped into a spatial map of size $H \times W$ and stacked with four complementary statistics so as to form a $C$-channel feature frame $\mathbf{F}_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ with $C = 5$. The multi-channel design ensures that diverse aspects of the update are simultaneously observable, which, as we shall see, is essential for distinguishing the three attack families introduced in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}.

\subsubsection{Per-Round Normalization Statistics}
Let $\{\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\}_{k \in S_t}$ be the latent codes of all $|S_t|$ participants in round~$t$. We compute the following aggregate statistics over the participant population:
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^v &= \frac{1}{|S_t|} \sum_k \mathbf{z}_{k,t}, &\quad \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^v &= \text{std}_k(\mathbf{z}_{k,t}), \label{eq:stats_v}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^m &= \frac{1}{|S_t|} \sum_k |\mathbf{z}_{k,t}|, &\quad \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^m &= \text{std}_k(|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}|), \label{eq:stats_m}\\
\mu_t^N &= \frac{1}{|S_t|} \sum_k \|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\|_2, &\quad \sigma_t^N &= \text{std}_k(\|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\|_2), \label{eq:stats_n}\\
\mu_t^C &= \text{mean}_k(c_{k,t}), &\quad \sigma_t^C &= \text{std}_k(c_{k,t}), \label{eq:stats_c}
\end{align}
and the cosine similarity of each latent code with respect to the population mean direction:
\begin{equation}
c_{k,t} = \frac{\mathbf{z}_{k,t}^\top \boldsymbol{\mu}_t^v}{\|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\|_2 \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^v\|_2}.
\label{eq:cosine}
\end{equation}
All of the above are computed across the participant population of the current round, so that every channel of the feature frame is a \emph{relative} quantity that the server derives itself.

\subsubsection{Five-Channel Feature Frame}
The feature frame $\mathbf{F}_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{5 \times H \times W}$ is assembled from five channels, each obtained by reshaping a $K$-dimensional vector into an $H \times W$ spatial map:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}^{(1)} &= \frac{\mathbf{z}_{k,t} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t^v}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^v}, \label{eq:ch1}\\
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}^{(2)} &= \text{sign}(\mathbf{z}_{k,t}), \label{eq:ch2}\\
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}^{(3)} &= \frac{|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}| - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t^m}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^m}, \label{eq:ch3}\\
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}^{(4)} &= \hat{N}_{k,t} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{H \times W}, \quad \hat{N}_{k,t} = \frac{\|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\|_2 - \mu_t^N}{\sigma_t^N}, \label{eq:ch4}\\
\mathbf{F}_{k,t}^{(5)} &= \hat{c}_{k,t} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{H \times W}, \quad \hat{c}_{k,t} = \frac{c_{k,t} - \mu_t^C}{\sigma_t^C}. \label{eq:ch5}
\end{align}
Channels~\eqref{eq:ch1}--\eqref{eq:ch3} encode spatially resolved latent deviation, sign structure, and magnitude deviation of the update. Channels~\eqref{eq:ch4} and~\eqref{eq:ch5} provide broadcast scalar features capturing update-norm and directional alignment anomalies.

It is worth emphasizing what the frame deliberately excludes. A natural sixth channel would be the change in each client's local validation loss, $\Delta\ell_{k,t}$, which appears attractive because it links the latent-space geometry to the client's learning dynamics. We do not use it. Such a quantity is \emph{self-reported}: it is computed on the client's own data and transmitted by the client itself, so an adversary is free to fabricate it and can drive the corresponding channel to whatever value minimizes its own anomaly score. Admitting an attacker-controllable input into the detector would create precisely the kind of single-signal bypass that Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} criticizes in prior defenses. Every channel in~\eqref{eq:ch1}--\eqref{eq:ch5} is instead computed by the server from the submitted update alone, and is therefore outside the adversary's direct control.

\subsubsection{Temporal History Buffer}
For each client $k$, the BS maintains a sliding history buffer of the last $H_{\max}$ frames, namely $\mathcal{H}_{k,t} = [\mathbf{F}_{k,t-H_{\max}+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{F}_{k,t}]$. A temporal window of length~$T$ is then extracted as the most recent $T$ consecutive frames, yielding a sequence tensor $\mathbf{S}_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C \times H \times W}$ that serves as input to the ConvLSTM forecaster.

\subsection{Per-Round Statistical Anomaly Detector}
\label{sec:stat_detector}

To keep the framework responsive in early rounds before the ConvLSTM forecaster has accumulated sufficient temporal context, we propose a per-round statistical detector that requires no temporal history. Inspired by the observation that, by the Byzantine assumption $M < N/2$, the coordinate-wise median of the participant population always remains a robust, benign reference, the detector measures the deviation of every participant from this reference along five complementary mathematical axes. It is worth noting that these axes are not chosen with any specific attack family in mind; rather, they are selected because they describe a distinct geometric or statistical feature of an update vector magnitude, direction, displacement, sign structure, and inter-client redundancy and any misbehavior that perturbs one of these features will manifest in the corresponding score, irrespective of the underlying generation mechanism. Notably, the use of the median in place of the mean is critical, since the mean itself is shifted by the very malicious clients we attempt to detect.

Let $\{\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\}_{k\in S_t}$ denote the latent codes of the round-$t$ participants and let $\bar{\mathbf{z}}_t = \mathrm{median}_{k \in S_t}(\mathbf{z}_{k,t})$ be the coordinate-wise median reference. Let $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,t}^{(\mathcal{L})}$ denote the layer-filtered raw update of client~$k$ defined in~\eqref{eq:layer_update}, and let $\mathcal{N}_t^{\mathrm{trim}} = \mathrm{median}\bigl(\bigl\{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j,t}^{(\mathcal{L})}\|_2\bigr\}_{j \in B_t^{\mathrm{trim}}}\bigr)$ be the trimmed median over the bottom-$80\%$ of the participant norms, which is used as a robust benign norm reference.

\subsubsection{Norm Score}
The norm score quantifies the magnitude of deviation of a client update relative to the participant population. Since any perturbation that drives the update norm well beyond the natural scale of the benign cluster registers as an outlier in $\ell_2$ magnitude, we adopt a log-ratio formulation that is insensitive to small fluctuations yet saturates rapidly under large inflation, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t}^{(N)} = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{1}{5}\,\log_2\max\!\left(1,\, \frac{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,t}^{(\mathcal{L})}\|_2}{\mathcal{N}_t^{\mathrm{trim}}}\right)\right) \in [0,1].
\label{eq:norm_score}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Cosine Score}
The cosine score quantifies the angular deviation of a client update from the population reference direction. Specifically, any update whose orientation drifts away from or opposes the median direction of the participant cluster registers a large angular discrepancy, which is given by
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t}^{(C)} = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(1 - \frac{\mathbf{z}_{k,t}^{\!\top} \bar{\mathbf{z}}_t}{\|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\|_2 \, \|\bar{\mathbf{z}}_t\|_2}\right) \in [0,1],
\label{eq:cos_score}
\end{equation}
which maps the latent cosine similarity from $[-1,1]$ to $[0,1]$, with anti-aligned updates approaching unity.

\subsubsection{Distance Score}
The distance score quantifies the Euclidean displacement of a client's latent code from the population reference and is therefore complementary to the cosine score, which only measures orientation. To suppress the influence of natural client heterogeneity, we adopt a robust z-score based on the median absolute deviation (MAD), which is calculated as
\begin{align}
d_{k,t} &= \|\mathbf{z}_{k,t} - \bar{\mathbf{z}}_t\|_2, \quad \tilde{d}_t = \mathrm{median}_k(d_{k,t}), \label{eq:dist_raw}\\
\mathrm{MAD}_t^{(d)} &= \mathrm{median}_k\bigl(|d_{k,t} - \tilde{d}_t|\bigr), \label{eq:dist_mad}\\
r_{k,t}^{(D)} &= \tanh\!\left(\frac{0.6745 \, \max(0, d_{k,t}-\tilde{d}_t)}{2\,\mathrm{MAD}_t^{(d)}}\right). \label{eq:dist_score}
\end{align}
It is worth noting that the constant $0.6745$~\cite{nist_modified_zscore} converts the MAD into an estimate of the underlying standard deviation under the Gaussian model, while the $\tanh$ saturation continuously bounds the score to $[0,1]$.

\subsubsection{Sign Score}
The sign score quantifies per-coordinate sign disagreement between a client update and the population reference, weighted by the local amplitude of the reference. By emphasizing the high-amplitude coordinates of the benign median, the score remains insensitive to noise on near-zero coordinates yet sharply responds to systematic sign reversals along the dominant directions of the population, namely,
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t}^{(S)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{K} |\bar z_{t,i}|\,\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{sgn}(z_{k,t,i}) \neq \mathrm{sgn}(\bar z_{t,i})\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{K} |\bar z_{t,i}| + \epsilon},
\label{eq:sign_score}
\end{equation}
so that $r_{k,t}^{(S)}$ rises only when the sign disagreement aligns with the high-amplitude coordinates of the benign median.

\subsubsection{Clone Score}
The clone score quantifies inter-client redundancy by measuring how closely a client update resembles its closest peer in the population. Whereas the previous four scores compare each client to the population reference, this score compares clients to one another and therefore captures coordinated submissions whose pairwise similarity exceeds anything attributable to ordinary data heterogeneity. Let $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k,t} = \mathbf{z}_{k,t} / \|\mathbf{z}_{k,t}\|_2$. We compute the maximum off-diagonal cosine similarity for each client and rescale it through a hinge transformation, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_{k,t} &= \max_{j \in S_t,\, j \neq k} \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k,t}^{\!\top} \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{j,t}, \\
r_{k,t}^{(L)} &= \min\!\left(1,\, \max\!\left(0,\, \frac{m_{k,t} - \tau_c}{1 - \tau_c}\right)\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:clone_score}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_c = 0.95$ is the clone-similarity threshold. Notably, benign clients remain below $\tau_c$ in practice, since heterogeneous local datasets cause their normalized updates to disperse, whereas any group of clients submitting near-identical vectors approaches a similarity of unity and thus saturates $r_{k,t}^{(L)}$ to one.

\subsubsection{Statistical Score Fusion and Smoothing}
The five complementary signals are linearly combined into a single statistical anomaly score, which is given by
\begin{equation}
r_{k,t} = \beta_N r_{k,t}^{(N)} + \beta_C r_{k,t}^{(C)} + \beta_D r_{k,t}^{(D)} + \beta_S r_{k,t}^{(S)} + \beta_L r_{k,t}^{(L)},
\label{eq:stat_score}
\end{equation}
with non-negative weights $\sum_i \beta_i = 1$. Since each constituent score isolates a distinct mathematical feature of an update magnitude, direction, displacement, sign structure, and inter-client redundancy, a misbehaving client that distorts any one of these features is reflected in the fused score, regardless of the underlying mechanism by which the distortion was generated. We use the fixed weights
\begin{equation}
(\beta_N, \beta_C, \beta_D, \beta_S, \beta_L) = (0.30,\, 0.20,\, 0.25,\, 0.15,\, 0.10),
\label{eq:beta_values}
\end{equation}
which place the greatest mass on the two axes that respond to the largest number of attack families (norm inflation and MAD-normalized displacement) and the least on the clone score, which is informative only against coordinated submissions. These weights are set a priori rather than tuned per dataset or per attack; no attack-specific calibration is performed anywhere in the framework. To dampen transient fluctuations caused by non-IID drift, the statistical score is then EWMA-smoothed across rounds, namely,
\begin{equation}
\bar r_{k,t} = \lambda_r\,\bar r_{k,t-1} + (1-\lambda_r)\,r_{k,t},
\label{eq:stat_ewma}
\end{equation}
with decay $\lambda_r = 0.4$. This arm is smoothed more lightly than the temporal arm of Section~\ref{sec:convlstm_ewma}, whose decay is $\lambda = 0.6$: the statistical score is available for every client at every round and is therefore allowed to react quickly, whereas the ConvLSTM score is refreshed only intermittently and benefits from greater inertia. Hence, $\bar r_{k,t}$ provides a low-cost, history-light statistical estimate of how anomalous client~$k$ has been in the recent past.

\subsection{ConvLSTM-EWMA Anomaly Scoring}
\label{sec:convlstm_ewma}

\subsubsection{ConvLSTM Forecaster}
To capture the joint spatial structure and temporal evolution of the feature frames produced in the previous subsection, we employ a single-layer ConvLSTM forecaster~\cite{shi2015convolutional} that processes the spatial feature sequence $\mathbf{S}_{k,t}$ and predicts the next frame in a self-supervised, next-frame prediction fashion. At each time step $\tau$, the ConvLSTM cell updates its hidden state $\mathbf{h}_\tau$ and cell state $\mathbf{c}_\tau$ as
\begin{align}
\mathbf{i}_\tau &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_{xi} * \mathbf{F}_\tau + \mathbf{W}_{hi} * \mathbf{h}_{\tau-1} + \mathbf{b}_i), \label{eq:lstm_i}\\
\mathbf{f}_\tau &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_{xf} * \mathbf{F}_\tau + \mathbf{W}_{hf} * \mathbf{h}_{\tau-1} + \mathbf{b}_f), \label{eq:lstm_f}\\
\mathbf{o}_\tau &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_{xo} * \mathbf{F}_\tau + \mathbf{W}_{ho} * \mathbf{h}_{\tau-1} + \mathbf{b}_o), \label{eq:lstm_o}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_\tau &= \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{xg} * \mathbf{F}_\tau + \mathbf{W}_{hg} * \mathbf{h}_{\tau-1} + \mathbf{b}_g), \label{eq:lstm_g}\\
\mathbf{c}_\tau &= \mathbf{f}_\tau \odot \mathbf{c}_{\tau-1} + \mathbf{i}_\tau \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_\tau, \label{eq:lstm_c}\\
\mathbf{h}_\tau &= \mathbf{o}_\tau \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_\tau), \label{eq:lstm_h}
\end{align}
where $*$ denotes spatial convolution, $\odot$ the element-wise product, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ the sigmoid activation. After processing the context frames $\mathbf{F}_{k,t-T+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{F}_{k,t-1}$, the final hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{T-1}$ is passed through a $1 \times 1$ output convolution to produce the predicted frame $\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$. Accordingly, the raw anomaly score for client $k$ at round $t$ is the mean squared prediction error over all spatial and channel dimensions, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
s_{k,t} = \frac{1}{C \cdot H \cdot W} \|\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{k,t} - \mathbf{F}_{k,t}\|_F^2.
\label{eq:anomaly_score}
\end{equation}

The detector parameters $\theta$ are updated in a semi-online fashion using a replay buffer $\mathcal{B}_{\text{win}}$ of clean temporal windows collected from rounds in which the global model has demonstrated consistent performance improvement. The self-supervised training objective minimizes the next-frame prediction loss, which is given by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{\text{win}}|} \sum_{\mathbf{S} \in \mathcal{B}_{\text{win}}} \|f_\theta(\mathbf{S}_{1:T-1}) - \mathbf{S}_T\|_F^2,
\label{eq:det_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{S}_{1:T-1}$ denotes the first $T-1$ frames of a window and $f_\theta$ is the ConvLSTM forecaster. By restricting parameter updates to rounds with verified performance gains, the framework avoids inadvertently fitting the detector to feature patterns associated with Byzantine activity, an effect that would otherwise emerge under sustained, low-magnitude poisoning such as the LIE attack.

Concretely, a round is deemed \emph{good} when the global model has improved in either accuracy or loss for two consecutive rounds. Windows are then admitted to $\mathcal{B}_{\text{win}}$ only from clients that survived the filter of Section~\ref{sec:dual_threshold} in that round and whose fused score falls below the population median, capped at three windows per client so that no single client dominates the buffer. Updates are performed at most once every two rounds, and only once $|\mathcal{B}_{\text{win}}|$ exceeds a minimum occupancy, using Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$ for at most $50$ minibatch steps. The detector is randomly initialized and is trained purely online: no pre-training on external data is performed at any point.

\subsubsection{EWMA Smoothing and Adaptive Scoring Policy}
Evaluating the ConvLSTM for every client at every round incurs $\mathcal{O}(|S_t|)$ forward passes and constitutes a significant overhead for a large client population. To reduce this cost while maintaining responsiveness to emerging threats, we apply EWMA smoothing together with a selective re-evaluation policy that triggers ConvLSTM inference only when necessary. The EWMA score for client $k$ is maintained as
\begin{equation}
e_{k,t} =
\begin{cases}
\lambda \, e_{k,t-1} + (1 - \lambda) \, s_{k,t} & \text{if $k$ is re-evaluated at $t$,}\\
\lambda \, e_{k,t-1} & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ewma}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \in (0,1)$ is the decay factor. It is worth noting that the EWMA provides temporal inertia: a single anomalous round raises the score only gradually, while sustained benign behavior causes it to decay, thereby dampening the false alarms induced by transient communication artifacts.

It is worth being explicit about the second branch of~\eqref{eq:ewma}, since it has a consequence that we quantify empirically in Section~\ref{sec:quantile_sweep}. A client that is \emph{not} re-evaluated does not retain its previous score: the score is decayed geometrically toward zero. This is deliberate, in that stale evidence of anomaly should not persist indefinitely, but it also means that the temporal score of a client carries information about \emph{how recently it was examined}, and not only about how anomalous it was.

Accordingly, the re-evaluation policy operates in two phases. During an initial cold-start phase of $T_{\mathrm{cs}}$ rounds, all participating clients are scored, without any budget cap, so as to build a reliable EWMA baseline. After cold-start, re-evaluation of client $k$ is triggered under either of the following conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Periodic refresh:} client $k$ has not been scored for more than $R_{\max}$ consecutive rounds;
    \item \textit{High-EWMA trigger:} the current EWMA score $e_{k,t-1}$ exceeds a dynamic threshold $\tau_s$, which is defined as the $q_s$-th empirical quantile of all currently tracked EWMA scores, namely,
    \begin{equation}
    \tau_s = \text{Quantile}_{q_s}\!\left(\{e_{k,t-1}\}_{k \in S_t}\right).
    \label{eq:trigger_threshold}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

In addition, a round-level adaptive scoring cap $B_t$ further bounds the total number of clients evaluated per round. The cap is driven by the normalized inter-quartile range (IQR) of the current EWMA distribution, which serves as a proxy for the overall level of client heterogeneity and threat activity, and is given by
\begin{equation}
\nu_t = \frac{\mathrm{IQR}(\{e_{k,t-1}\})}{|\mathrm{median}(\{e_{k,t-1}\})| + \epsilon},
\label{eq:iqr_ratio}
\end{equation}
which, after saturation to the unit interval, is linearly interpolated between a floor $B_{\min}$ and a ceiling $B_{\max}$, namely,
\begin{equation}
B_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = B_{\min} + (B_{\max} - B_{\min}) \cdot \min(\nu_t, 1),
\label{eq:scoring_cap}
\end{equation}
and then smoothed across rounds, as $B_t = \alpha_B B_{t-1} + (1-\alpha_B) B_t^{\mathrm{raw}}$ with $\alpha_B = 0.7$, so that the budget does not oscillate with round-to-round noise in the score distribution. When the number of triggered clients exceeds $B_t$, only those with the highest current EWMA scores are re-evaluated, so that the most suspicious clients are prioritized within the allotted computational budget. As a result, the proposed ConvLSTM-EWMA detector inherits the responsiveness of full per-round inference while keeping the per-round complexity bounded by $B_t$ rather than by $|S_t|$.

We stress that $q_s$ is a \emph{computational} control, not a detection threshold: it determines which clients receive a fresh ConvLSTM forward pass, and it never appears in the filtering rule of Section~\ref{sec:dual_threshold}. Its effect on detection is therefore indirect, acting through the staleness of the temporal scores, and Section~\ref{sec:quantile_sweep} measures exactly that effect.

\subsubsection{Two-Sided Relative Score for LIE Polarity Compensation}
A direct use of the EWMA-smoothed score $e_{k,t}$ defined in~\eqref{eq:ewma} is, however, problematic against the LIE attack: because LIE crafts the same poisoned vector across rounds, the ConvLSTM forecaster rapidly learns to predict it, leading to \emph{lower} rather than higher prediction errors for the malicious clients than for the honest ones. The polarity of the score is therefore inverted under LIE, and any single-sided upper-tail filter built on $e_{k,t}$ would systematically misclassify benign clients as malicious and vice versa. Inspired by this observation, we do not use $e_{k,t}$ directly; instead, we transform it into a two-sided relative score that simultaneously flags clients whose EWMA is anomalously high \emph{or} anomalously low compared to the participant population.

Specifically, let $\tilde{e}_t = \mathrm{median}_{k\in S_t}(e_{k,t})$ and $\mathrm{MAD}_t^{(e)} = \mathrm{median}_{k\in S_t}\bigl(|e_{k,t}-\tilde{e}_t|\bigr)$ denote, respectively, the round-$t$ median and the MAD of the EWMA scores. We define the upper- and lower-tail robust z-scores as
\begin{align}
z_{k,t}^{\uparrow} &= \frac{0.6745\,\max(0,\, e_{k,t}-\tilde{e}_t)}{\mathrm{MAD}_t^{(e)}+\epsilon}, \label{eq:z_up}\\
z_{k,t}^{\downarrow} &= \frac{0.6745\,\max(0,\, \tilde{e}_t-e_{k,t})}{\mathrm{MAD}_t^{(e)}+\epsilon}, \label{eq:z_down}
\end{align}
and combine them into a two-sided relative score, namely,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{k,t} = \tanh\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\max\!\bigl(z_{k,t}^{\uparrow},\, z_{k,t}^{\downarrow}\bigr)\right) \in [0,1].
\label{eq:rho_score}
\end{equation}
By construction, $\rho_{k,t} \to 0$ when $e_{k,t}$ is close to the population median, $\rho_{k,t} \to 1$ when $e_{k,t}$ deviates strongly in either tail, and the polarity inversion induced by LIE is automatically resolved without any prior knowledge of the attack family.

\subsubsection{Score Fusion via Max-Pooling}
To exploit the complementarity of the per-round statistical detector developed in Section~\ref{sec:stat_detector} and the temporal ConvLSTM detector, we fuse the EWMA-smoothed statistical score $\bar r_{k,t}$ in~\eqref{eq:stat_ewma} and the two-sided relative ConvLSTM score $\rho_{k,t}$ in~\eqref{eq:rho_score} via max-pooling, namely,
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{k,t} = \max\!\bigl(\bar r_{k,t},\; \rho_{k,t}\bigr).
\label{eq:fused_score}
\end{equation}
Max-pooling preserves the principle that either arm can independently flag a client. In particular, the statistical detector reacts from the very first post-warm-up round, before sufficient temporal context has accumulated, while the relative ConvLSTM score becomes effective once the EWMA distribution stabilizes. Hence, the fused score $\Phi_{k,t}$ is robust across both early and late stages of training and against all three attack families introduced in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}.
\subsection{Dual-Threshold Robust Aggregation}
\label{sec:dual_threshold}

\subsubsection{Adaptive Quantile Filtering}
Once the fused scores $\{\Phi_{k,t}\}_{k\in S_t}$ in~\eqref{eq:fused_score} have been updated, the BS determines a per-round quantile threshold $\tau_t^{(q)}$ via an adaptive quantile $q_t \in [q_{\min}, q_{\max}]$, namely,
\begin{equation}
\tau_t^{(q)} = \text{Quantile}_{q_t}\!\left(\{\Phi_{k,t}\}_{k \in S_t}\right).
\label{eq:filt_threshold}
\end{equation}
The quantile $q_t$ is, however, not fixed: it is adjusted each round by combining four complementary signals into a scalar strictness index $\phi_t \in [0,1]$, which is given by
\begin{equation}
\phi_t = w_{\mathrm{spr}} \phi_t^{\mathrm{spr}} + w_{\mathrm{sev}} \phi_t^{\mathrm{sev}} + w_{\mathrm{srv}} \phi_t^{\mathrm{srv}} + w_{\mathrm{drp}} \phi_t^{\mathrm{drp}},
\label{eq:strictness}
\end{equation}
with the fixed weights
\begin{equation}
(w_{\mathrm{spr}}, w_{\mathrm{sev}}, w_{\mathrm{srv}}, w_{\mathrm{drp}}) = (0.35,\, 0.30,\, 0.20,\, 0.15),
\label{eq:w_values}
\end{equation}
which sum to one. Each component captures a distinct facet of the current threat landscape, as summarized below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\phi_t^{\mathrm{spr}}$ (\emph{spread signal}): the relative IQR of the current fused scores, which measures the degree of separation between high- and low-scoring clients. A wide spread indicates active attack behavior and warrants stricter filtering. Writing $u_t$ for that relative IQR, the signal is saturated as $\phi_t^{\mathrm{spr}} = u_t/(u_t+1) \in [0,1)$.
    \item $\phi_t^{\mathrm{sev}}$ (\emph{severity signal}): the ratio of the score mean to its standard deviation, which captures the overall intensity of anomalous activity across the participant population, saturated by the same $u/(u+1)$ map.
    \item $\phi_t^{\mathrm{srv}}$ (\emph{server signal}): derived from the consecutive-round improvement streak of the global model. It takes the value $0$ when the model has been improving for at least two consecutive rounds, $1$ when the streak is broken, and $0.5$ otherwise. Sustained improvement indicates that the current filtering level is effective, so that the filter can be relaxed.
    \item $\phi_t^{\mathrm{drp}}$ (\emph{drop-rate signal}): penalizes over-aggressive filtering. When the fraction of excluded clients in the previous round exceeded a target drop rate $\xi_{\mathrm{drop}}$, this component reduces the strictness so as to restore broader participation.
\end{itemize}

The quantile is then mapped from the strictness index as
\begin{equation}
q_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = q_{\max} - \phi_t\,(q_{\max} - q_{\min}),
\label{eq:q_raw}
\end{equation}
and smoothed across rounds so as to prevent oscillation, namely,
\begin{equation}
q_t = \alpha_q\, q_{t-1} + (1-\alpha_q)\, q_t^{\mathrm{raw}},
\label{eq:q_smooth}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_q \in (0,1)$ is a smoothing coefficient. Hence, the mapping in~\eqref{eq:q_raw} ensures that higher threat evidence (larger $\phi_t$) yields stricter filtering (smaller $q_t$, retaining fewer clients), while a benign or improving environment allows the filter to relax toward $q_{\max}$.

\subsubsection{Absolute-Threshold Backstop}
The adaptive quantile $q_t$ is, by construction, a \emph{relative} criterion: it always retains at least a fraction $q_t$ of the participants, irrespective of the absolute level of the fused score. Consequently, when the gap between the malicious and benign clusters is large for instance, under a large-magnitude Gaussian attack the quantile filter alone may admit a clearly malicious client whose fused score sits well above any reasonable benign level. To close this gap, we introduce a fixed absolute threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}} = 0.40$ and define the effective per-round threshold as the smaller of the two, namely,
\begin{equation}
\tau_t^{\mathrm{filt}} = \min\!\Bigl(\tau_t^{(q)},\; \tau_{\mathrm{abs}}\Bigr).
\label{eq:dual_thresh}
\end{equation}
The trusted subset is then identified as
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{S}_t = \bigl\{k \in S_t : \Phi_{k,t} \le \tau_t^{\mathrm{filt}}\bigr\}.
\label{eq:filtered_set}
\end{equation}
A minimum participation guard $|\widetilde{S}_t| \ge S_{\min}$ is enforced in line with constraint (P1b): when the dual-threshold filter would leave fewer than $S_{\min}$ participants, the $S_{\min}$ clients with the lowest $\Phi_{k,t}$ are reinstated. Notably, the setting $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}=1$ recovers the quantile-only filter as a special case, so that the absolute threshold acts as a backstop rather than as a primary filter.

\subsubsection{Filtered Aggregation}
The trusted subset $\widetilde{S}_t$ is finally passed to the server-side aggregation rule, namely,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}^{t+1} = \text{AGG}\!\left(\{(\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t}, n_k)\}_{k \in \widetilde{S}_t}\right).
\label{eq:filtered_agg}
\end{equation}
Since $\widetilde{S}_t \subseteq S_t$, the filtered aggregation in~\eqref{eq:filtered_agg} is a strict refinement of the corrupted-aggregation formulation in~\eqref{eq:poisoned_agg}: it is applicable to any choice of $\text{AGG}(\cdot)$, and the proposed anomaly-based pre-filtering is complementary to, and independently stackable with, any intrinsic robustness properties of the underlying aggregation rule. Throughout this work we deliberately instantiate $\text{AGG}(\cdot)$ as the plain data-weighted mean of FedAvg, with client~$k$ weighted by its local sample count $n_k$. No robust aggregator, trust score, or anomaly-proportional re-weighting is applied to the survivors: a client is either admitted to $\widetilde{S}_t$ in full or excluded from it entirely. All of the Byzantine robustness reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} is therefore attributable to the filter itself, and not to the aggregation rule. Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine} or the trimmed mean~\cite{yin2018byzantine} may be substituted for FedAvg without altering any other module, which would only add to the robustness established here.

\subsection{Algorithm Summary and Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{USTAD-FL: Unsupervised Spatio-Temporal Anomaly Detection at the BS}
\label{alg:ustad}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Initial model $\mathbf{w}^{0}$; warm-up length $T_w$; total rounds $T$; quantile range $[q_{\min},q_{\max}]$; absolute threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}$; minimum participation $S_{\min}$
\STATE Initialize SVD basis $\mathbf{B}$ and mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ from warm-up updates~\eqref{eq:centering}--\eqref{eq:svd}
\STATE Initialize ConvLSTM detector $f_\theta$, EWMA states $\{e_{k},\bar r_{k}\}$, and quantile $q_{0}\!=\!q_{\max}$
\FOR{$t = 1$ \TO $T_{\text{total}}$}
    \STATE Broadcast $\mathbf{w}^{t}$ to participants $S_t \subseteq \mathcal{K}$
    \STATE Receive updates $\{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t}\}_{k\in S_t}$
    \STATE \textit{// Latent projection and feature construction}
    \STATE Compute latent codes $\mathbf{z}_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:projection}; assemble feature frames $\mathbf{F}_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:ch1}--\eqref{eq:ch5}
    \STATE \textit{// Statistical detector}
    \STATE Compute statistical scores $r_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:norm_score}--\eqref{eq:stat_score}; update $\bar r_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:stat_ewma}
    \STATE \textit{// ConvLSTM detector with adaptive scoring policy}
    \STATE Determine scoring set $\mathcal{R}_t \subseteq S_t$ via the trigger conditions and budget cap~\eqref{eq:trigger_threshold}--\eqref{eq:scoring_cap}
    \FOR{each $k \in \mathcal{R}_t$}
        \STATE Compute $s_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:anomaly_score} and update $e_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:ewma}
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Compute relative score $\rho_{k,t}$ via~\eqref{eq:z_up}--\eqref{eq:rho_score}
    \STATE \textit{// Fusion and dual-threshold filtering}
    \STATE Fuse scores $\Phi_{k,t} \!=\! \max(\bar r_{k,t}, \rho_{k,t})$ via~\eqref{eq:fused_score}
    \STATE Update strictness index $\phi_t$~\eqref{eq:strictness} and quantile $q_t$~\eqref{eq:q_raw}--\eqref{eq:q_smooth}
    \STATE Compute $\tau_t^{\mathrm{filt}} = \min(\tau_t^{(q)}, \tau_{\mathrm{abs}})$~\eqref{eq:dual_thresh}; build $\widetilde{S}_t$~\eqref{eq:filtered_set}
    \IF{$|\widetilde{S}_t| < S_{\min}$}
        \STATE Reinstate the $S_{\min}$ clients with the lowest $\Phi_{k,t}$
    \ENDIF
    \STATE \textit{// Filtered aggregation}
    \STATE Update $\mathbf{w}^{t+1} \!\leftarrow\! \text{AGG}(\{(\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,t},n_k)\}_{k\in\widetilde{S}_t})$~\eqref{eq:filtered_agg}
    \STATE \textit{// Semi-online detector update on verified-gain rounds}
    \IF{the global accuracy improves by at least $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}$}
        \STATE Append the trusted windows to $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{win}}$ and update $\theta$ by minimizing~\eqref{eq:det_loss}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Final global model $\mathbf{w}^{T}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The complete USTAD-FL procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ustad}. Specifically, at each round, the BS executes (i) the SVD latent projection in~\eqref{eq:projection}, (ii) the spatio-temporal feature construction in~\eqref{eq:ch1}--\eqref{eq:ch5}, (iii) the per-round statistical detector in~\eqref{eq:norm_score}--\eqref{eq:stat_ewma}, (iv) the ConvLSTM-EWMA forecaster in~\eqref{eq:lstm_i}--\eqref{eq:rho_score}, (v) the score fusion in~\eqref{eq:fused_score}, and (vi) the dual-threshold filtered aggregation in~\eqref{eq:dual_thresh}--\eqref{eq:filtered_agg}. It is worth noting that the semi-online ConvLSTM update is performed only when the global model has demonstrated a verified accuracy gain, so that the detector is never trained on rounds suspected of poisoning.

As the proposed framework is executed at the BS only, we evaluate its per-round computational complexity from the BS perspective. The SVD projection requires $\mathcal{O}(|S_t|\,K\,D)$ multiplications, where $D$ is the layer-filtered parameter count and $K$ is the latent dimension of~\eqref{eq:latent_dim}. The five-signal statistical detector is dominated by the clone-similarity computation in~\eqref{eq:clone_score}, which is $\mathcal{O}(|S_t|^2 K)$ in the worst case but reduces to $\mathcal{O}(|S_t|^2)$ on the unit-normalized representation used in practice. The ConvLSTM forward pass costs $\mathcal{O}\bigl(B_t\,T\,(C + \eta)\,\eta\, H W k_h k_w\bigr)$, where $\eta$ is the hidden-channel width, $B_t$ is the per-round scoring budget of~\eqref{eq:scoring_cap}, and $k_h\!=\!k_w\!=\!3$. Because the gate convolution maps $C + \eta$ input channels to $4\eta$ outputs, and $\eta = 16$ exceeds $C = 5$ in our configuration, it is the hidden width rather than the channel count that dominates this term.

Two observations follow. First, the ConvLSTM cost is bounded by $B_t$ rather than by $|S_t|$, which is precisely what the re-evaluation policy of Section~\ref{sec:convlstm_ewma} exploits. Second, the overall per-round cost is nevertheless \emph{quadratic}, not linear, in the participant count, because of the pairwise clone score; the linear-in-$|S_t|$ terms are the projection and the four remaining statistical signals. For the federation sizes considered here the quadratic term is negligible, but it is the component that would govern the scaling behavior at substantially larger $|S_t|$, and it can be removed if required by computing the clone score on a random subsample of peers rather than on the full population.

\subsection{Security Analysis and Limitations}
We now examine USTAD-FL against an adversary aware of the detector itself and make explicit the assumptions on which its robustness rests.

\noindent\textit{Resistance to detector-aware adversaries.} The omniscient adversary defined in Section~\ref{sec:formulation} may attempt to evade the detector rather than only the aggregator. USTAD-FL resists such adaptation by design. First, the ConvLSTM forecaster is updated semi-online and only on rounds in which the global model exhibits a verified accuracy gain (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ustad}); the detector is therefore never trained on rounds suspected of poisoning, so an adversary cannot gradually teach it to accept malicious patterns as normal. Second, the statistical and temporal arms are fused by max-pooling, so an update must appear benign under \emph{every} spatial statistic \emph{and} remain consistent over time to escape detection; no single-statistic manipulation suffices. Third, the two-sided relative score in~\eqref{eq:rho_score} flags temporal behavior that is anomalously regular as well as anomalously irregular, neutralizing the score-polarity inversion that the LIE attack would otherwise exploit, while the clone score in~\eqref{eq:clone_score} penalizes the near-identical updates that aggregator-agnostic attacks such as Min-Max produce as a structural side effect.

\noindent\textit{Assumptions and limitations.} In common with the model-poisoning literature against which it is evaluated~\cite{baruch2019little, shejwalkar2021manipulating}, USTAD-FL assumes a short clean warm-up of $T_w$ rounds during which the SVD basis is fitted; an adversary that poisons from the very first round could bias this basis. The exposure is limited, however: $T_w$ is small (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) and, unlike history-based detectors that require a long benign trajectory, the statistical arm becomes effective from the first post-warm-up round. USTAD-FL further assumes that malicious clients remain a minority ($M < N/2$), as required by the coordinate-wise median reference. Relaxing the clean-warm-up assumption through robust basis estimation, defending against a malicious majority, and extending the spatio-temporal detector to targeted backdoor attacks are promising directions for future work.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We deploy our solution on a standard federated learning system built upon the Flower framework~\cite{beutel2020flower}. We evaluate on two popular benchmarks for federated learning: MNIST~\cite{lecun1998mnist} and Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion}. We set the total number of clients to $N = 100$, of which $40$ are compromised by the adversary. All clients participate in every round. Data are distributed across all clients using a Dirichlet distribution with $\alpha = 0.5$ to simulate a high degree of non-IID heterogeneity. At each client, we train a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) consistent with architectures used in FLDetector~\cite{zhang2022fldetector} and FLTrust~\cite{cao2020fltrust}. For honest clients, trained models are sent directly to the server; for malicious clients, each client executes the attack procedures described in~\cite{baruch2019little, fang2020local}. The adversary remains dormant during the $T_w = 5$ warm-up rounds and begins attacking at round~$6$, so that the detector must lock onto an attack that is already in progress rather than one it has observed from the outset.

Our evaluation comprises two experimental campaigns, which differ in training horizon and in the treatment of the re-evaluation quantile~$q_s$; both are reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Defense comparison} (Section~\ref{sec:defense_comparison}): $30$ communication rounds, $5$ local epochs, and a fixed re-evaluation quantile $q_s = 0.8$. USTAD-FL is compared against FLDetector~\cite{zhang2022fldetector}, FLTrust~\cite{cao2020fltrust}, and Multi-Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine}.
    \item \emph{Detection and efficiency study} (Sections~\ref{sec:detection_quality} and~\ref{sec:quantile_sweep}): $50$ communication rounds, $10$ local epochs, and $q_s$ swept over $\{0.0, 0.1, \ldots, 1.0\}$ on both datasets and all three attacks, yielding $66$ configurations in total.

\subsection{Impact of Byzantine Attacks on Vanilla FedAvg}

Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy_plots} shows the impact of Byzantine attacks on a vanilla FedAvg aggregator operating without any defense mechanism. Under all three attack variants (Gaussian, LIE, and Min-Max), the model undergoes catastrophic performance collapse immediately after the warm-up phase (round~5), with accuracy dropping to near-random levels ($\approx 0.10$) within a few rounds and remaining degraded for the entirety of the training horizon. The consistency of this collapse across all attack types and datasets confirms a fundamental vulnerability of FedAvg: without an anomaly-aware pre-filtering stage, a small fraction of Byzantine participants is sufficient to completely subvert global model convergence under non-IID data distributions.

\subsection{Defense Performance Comparison}

It is important to note that Multi-Krum operates under a privileged threat model in which the server is assumed to have prior knowledge of the exact number of malicious participants, an assumption that is rarely satisfied in real-world deployments; the remaining baselines, including the proposed method, operate without prior knowledge. Across all experimental scenarios, the proposed framework achieves consistent, monotonically increasing accuracy from the first communication round onward. As Table~\ref{tab:main_results} shows, USTAD-FL attains the highest final accuracy among all defenses in five of the six tabulated configurations, trails the privileged Multi-Krum by less than one percentage point on the sixth (MNIST under LIE), and matches the attack-free FedAvg reference to within one percentage point in every case, exceeding it on MNIST. This stable learning trajectory is a direct consequence of the spatio-temporal anomaly scoring mechanism, which accurately identifies and suppresses Byzantine contributions at each round without disrupting the aggregation of legitimate client updates.

FLDetector exhibits a characteristic failure mode consistent across all tested scenarios: its accuracy increases during the warm-up phase but collapses irreversibly immediately thereafter, converging to near-random performance for the remainder of training. Because FLDetector requires a prolonged warm-up period, it cannot detect attacks that begin early.

FLTrust suffers from pronounced training instability, exhibiting large round-to-round oscillations that persist throughout the full training horizon. We attribute this to the sensitivity of its trust-score weighting mechanism to the heterogeneous gradient distributions arising from non-IID data partitioning.

Multi-Krum, despite benefiting from privileged knowledge of the attacker count, exhibits markedly uneven performance across datasets. The substantial performance gap reveals that geometric outlier-rejection is poorly suited to non-IID settings, where the natural divergence among benign client updates cannot be distinguished from adversarial perturbations without temporal context. In lower-complexity scenarios (e.g., MNIST), Multi-Krum occasionally reaches accuracy comparable to our method; however, the proposed framework achieves equivalent results without any prior knowledge of the adversary and with consistently faster convergence in the early rounds.


\subsection{Detection Quality at the Operating Point}
\label{sec:detection_quality}

Accuracy is an indirect and, in this setting, rather insensitive proxy for detection: with $100$ clients the global model still converges even when a substantial number of benign updates are discarded, provided the attackers are excluded. We therefore measure the detector directly. Let $\mathrm{TP}$, $\mathrm{FP}$, $\mathrm{FN}$, and $\mathrm{TN}$ denote the per-round counts of correctly flagged malicious clients, wrongly flagged benign clients, missed malicious clients, and correctly retained benign clients. Fig.~\ref{fig:detection_tau06} reports two quantities per round at the recommended operating point $q_s = 0.6$: the \emph{detection rate} (recall) $\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$, and the \emph{false-detection rate} $\mathrm{FP}/N$ as plotted. We state the normalization explicitly because the latter is neither the statistical false-discovery rate $\mathrm{FP}/(\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TP})$ nor the false-positive rate $\mathrm{FP}/(\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN})$; where a claim depends on the benign population, we quote the benign rejection rate $\mathrm{FP}/(\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN})$ instead.

From round~$7$ onward the detection rate is exactly $1.000$ in five of the six configurations: every attacker is caught in every round. The sixth, Fashion-MNIST under Min-Max, attains $0.999$, missing a single malicious client in round~$13$. At the attack onset (round~$6$) the picture is different: the Gaussian attack is detected immediately at a rate of $1.000$ on both datasets, whereas LIE and Min-Max are only partially caught, at rates between $0.350$ and $0.525$. This is a one-round \emph{lock-on latency} rather than a defeat. A temporal detector scores a client against its own recent history, and at the first attacked round no malicious history yet exists for the attack to be scored against; the transient is mechanically inherent to the approach and is resolved by the following round. During the warm-up rounds $1$--$5$ no malicious client is present, so recall is undefined and is not plotted; the false-detection rate there is approximately $0.46$, reflecting a cold start in which the detector has no adversarial contrast against which to calibrate.

Once the detector has locked on, the false-detection rate settles low: averaged over the final ten rounds it lies between $0.010$ and $0.037$ in five of the six configurations. One configuration does not follow this pattern, and we report it explicitly rather than averaging it away: MNIST under Min-Max settles at $0.146$. In that case the flagged benign clients are re-admitted in later rounds rather than being permanently excluded, which is why its accuracy remains in line with the others. We emphasize that these are properties of the \emph{fused} detector; the present experiments do not isolate the contribution of the temporal arm from that of the statistical arm, and we therefore make no claim about which arm is responsible for a given detection.

\subsection{Re-evaluation Quantile: Computation, Detection, and Precision}
\label{sec:quantile_sweep}

Fig.~\ref{fig:computation_reduction} reports the sensitivity of USTAD-FL to the re-evaluation trigger quantile~$q_s$ across both benchmarks and all three attack strategies, showing jointly the final-round global accuracy (solid curves, left axis) and the total number of ConvLSTM forward passes accumulated over training (dotted curves, right axis), the latter being a direct proxy for the cost of the temporal detector arm. Because the forward-pass count is governed by the scoring policy rather than by the data, the dotted curves for all six configurations nearly coincide and fall monotonically with~$q_s$, from approximately $3{,}520$ passes at $q_s = 0$ to approximately $2{,}320$ at $q_s = 0.6$ and approximately $820$ at $q_s = 1.0$. Raising the trigger from its most permissive setting to $q_s = 0.6$ therefore eliminates approximately $34\%$ of all ConvLSTM forward passes, and the most aggressive setting eliminates approximately $77\%$.

These savings cost nothing in accuracy. In \emph{all six} dataset--attack configurations the final accuracy is flat in~$q_s$: within any one configuration it varies by less than five accuracy points across the entire sweep, the largest such variation being $4.6$ points for Fashion-MNIST under LIE. Pooling the three attacks, MNIST remains within $[0.932, 0.960]$ and Fashion-MNIST within $[0.772, 0.825]$. Nor do the savings cost anything in \emph{recall}. Across all $66$ configurations of the sweep the detection rate never falls below $0.983$, and the evasion rate plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:asr_fdr_sweep} is indistinguishable from zero at every~$q_s$. Starving the temporal detector of forward passes does not let attackers through.

The savings are not free, however: they are paid for in \emph{precision}. The dashed curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:asr_fdr_sweep} show that the false-detection rate grows as the trigger is tightened. Under the Gaussian attack---the only configuration whose curve is clean and consistent across both datasets---the benign rejection rate roughly triples over the sweep, rising from $0.078$ to $0.212$ on MNIST and from $0.080$ to $0.225$ on Fashion-MNIST as $q_s$ moves from $0$ to $1$. Under the LIE and Min-Max attacks the same quantity is erratic, alternating between roughly $0.08$ and $0.55$ at adjacent values of~$q_s$; these are single-run measurements, and we deliberately refrain from reading a trend into them, noting only that their largest values also occur at large~$q_s$.

The mechanism behind this loss of precision is a property of the re-evaluation policy itself, and we state it plainly because it bounds the efficiency claim. When a client is not re-scored in a given round, its ConvLSTM EWMA is \emph{decayed} rather than held, so that clients which are repeatedly skipped drift geometrically toward zero. The two-sided relative score of Section~\ref{sec:method} was introduced precisely to catch LIE, whose clients score anomalously \emph{low}; a benign client that has simply not been re-evaluated for several rounds therefore comes to resemble a LIE attacker. Throttling thus manufactures false positives at exactly the clients it declines to examine. Holding the EWMA constant for unscored clients, or restricting the low branch of the two-sided score to clients that were actually re-evaluated in the current round, would decouple ``not recently scored'' from ``anomalously low''; we leave this correction, and the re-run of the sweep that would validate it, to future work.

Taken together, these results reframe the role of~$q_s$. It is not a robustness dial---recall is invariant to it---but a trade between computation and precision. We accordingly recommend $q_s = 0.6$ as the operating point: it captures approximately $34\%$ of the attainable savings while the benign rejection rate on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST stays near its floor in five of the six configurations, at $0.100$--$0.124$. The sixth, MNIST under Min-Max, is elevated at $0.279$, and is the same configuration that shows the higher steady-state false-detection rate in Fig.~\ref{fig:detection_tau06}. We note for completeness that the measured precision floor extends only to $q_s \approx 0.4$, so a deployment that values precision above computation should choose a smaller trigger; and that pushing $q_s$ to $1.0$, while attractive on compute grounds, roughly triples benign rejection and is therefore not recommended, even though the accuracy curves alone would not reveal the cost.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%=============================================================================

In this paper, we proposed USTAD-FL, an unsupervised spatio-temporal anomaly detection framework for Byzantine-robust federated learning. USTAD-FL models client updates as continuous spatio-temporal signals and scores each client with two complementary detectors---a low-cost per-round statistical detector and a ConvLSTM next-frame forecaster---whose fused score is filtered by a dual adaptive-quantile/absolute threshold before aggregation. An EWMA-based adaptive scoring policy concentrates ConvLSTM inference on the most suspicious clients, reducing the total number of forward passes by approximately 34\% at the recommended operating point without any loss of detection recall.

Under three representative untargeted attacks (Gaussian, LIE, and Min-Max), USTAD-FL matches or exceeds three state-of-the-art defenses on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, and its detection rate is at least $0.999$ from the round after the attack begins, subject to a one-round lock-on latency that is inherent to temporal detection. In contrast to existing approaches, it requires no prior knowledge of the number of malicious clients, no clean server-side dataset, and no assumption on the attack type, and it remains robust under high non-IID heterogeneity at a 40\% adversarial fraction. Our analysis of the re-evaluation trigger also isolates the one place where the efficiency gain is not free: recall is invariant to the trigger, but benign-client rejection grows as the temporal detector is throttled, because the scores of clients that are skipped decay into the very region the detector uses to recognize LIE. Future work therefore includes holding rather than decaying the scores of unscored clients and re-validating the efficiency claim against the corrected policy; an ablation isolating the temporal arm from the statistical arm, which the present experiments do not separate; extending the evaluation to more complex benchmarks such as CIFAR-10, and to multi-seed runs with error bars; relaxing the clean warm-up assumption through robust basis estimation; defending against a malicious majority; and extending the detector to targeted backdoor attacks.
